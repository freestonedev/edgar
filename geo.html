The short version

When buyers ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Gemini about remote engineering talent, the brand I run GEO for, a Y Combinator-backed company, shows up in roughly 1 of every 4 answers across 700+ tracked prompts, up from 1 in 5 in March, and it is the most-cited brand in its category by more than 2 to 1. None of that happened by accident. It's a weekly loop I run with one tool and one copywriter: Gauge shows where the AI engines look, we decide what they find.

Mention rate (visibility)

How often the brand is named in AI answers to tracked prompts. Ask the engines 100 of the questions real buyers ask, and if the brand appears in 25 of the answers, mention rate is 25%. It's the AI-era equivalent of ranking on page one.

Citation rate (trust)

How often the brand's own website is used as a cited source in those answers. A mention means the AI knows you exist; a citation means the engine is showing its receipts and pointing readers at your pages. Citations are what content strategy can directly earn.

Why GEO matters now

The way people find companies is changing underneath us. A buyer used to type a query into Google and click through ten blue links; now they ask ChatGPT or Gemini a question and get one answer. If your brand is in that answer, you just earned a lead you didn't pay for. If it isn't, a competitor did. There is no page two.

That discipline, earning presence in AI answers the way SEO earns presence in search results, is Generative Engine Optimization. The win condition is different from SEO in one crucial way: an AI gives the user a conclusion, not a list of links, so the goal isn't the click. It's being the brand the answer names and the source the engine cites. GEO today is where SEO was in 2003: real, measurable, and mostly unclaimed, because almost nobody has operational experience in it yet. I do, and here's what that actually looks like.

How I solved it

  1. Instrument before optimizing. Using Gauge, we built a library of tracked prompts, the questions real buyers ask AI assistants about our category, and started measuring how often the brand appears in answers across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, and Google AI Overviews, and which sources those engines cite.
  2. Study what the engines already trust. Citation analysis showed which domains and page types AI engines pull from in our category. That told us what to build: authoritative, question-shaped content with clean structure, not more generic blog posts.
  3. Create with a human in the loop. AI drafts fast, and AI also hallucinates. Every piece runs through a senior copywriter for factual accuracy, sourcing, and brand voice before it ships. Speed from the machine, trust from the human. That division of labor is the whole reason the content earns citations instead of burning credibility.
  4. Update before you create. The highest ROI in GEO is usually not a new page; it's an existing page restructured so an engine can lift from it: direct answers up top, schema markup, clear headings, current data, and honest sourcing. New content fills gaps; updates compound what already ranks.
  5. Measure weekly, adjust monthly. AI visibility sits in the same weekly report as GSC and GA4, so movement gets noticed in days. What earns citations gets more investment; what doesn't gets rewritten or retired.

The SEO foundation underneath

GEO doesn't replace SEO; it sits on top of it. AI engines read the same web Google crawls, and they reward the same fundamentals: schema markup, clean information architecture, internal linking, fast pages, and content that actually answers the question in the first screen. The publishing systems I build treat those as properties of the build, not a checklist, so every page ships discoverable by both kinds of engine. If your SEO house isn't in order, GEO is a roof with no walls.

Proving it's repeatable: Flylords

A playbook only counts if it works outside your own building. I ran the same system for Flylords, an outdoor media brand, as a four-week onboarding engagement with their founder and chief editor. In four weeks their team went from zero GEO practice to independently running it: how to create new content that AI engines cite, and, just as important, how to update their existing archive for maximum bang for buck, restructuring proven pages instead of always chasing net-new. The goal wasn't to do their GEO for them. It was to make their editorial team self-sufficient in it, and by week four, it was theirs.
